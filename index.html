Before you start: please read

This coda covers recent years, from 2017 to 2025. Many of the people who took part in these events, and the communities named in the official records, are living. This file uses official public papers only. It shows no photographs of people. It does not quote the Uluru Statement from the Heart, which is in copyright; you can read that statement at the custodians’ site, linked in this file. It does not use secret or sacred material, ceremony, or rock art.

Some official records describe injustice. It is all right to stop. If anything here is upsetting, tell your teacher or a trusted adult, or talk to one of these services. They are free and confidential.

Numbers checked on 22 November 2026.

Between December 2016 and May 2017 the Referendum Council held First Nations Regional Dialogues across Australia. Delegates then gathered at Uluru for the 2017 National Constitutional Convention, and made a statement. In 2023 voters were asked to approve a proposed law to alter the Constitution. The Australian Electoral Commission recorded the result. In May 2021 the Victorian Government, in partnership with the First Peoples’ Assembly of Victoria, set up the Yoorrook Justice Commission. Its final report, Yoorrook: Truth Be Told, was released on 1 July 2025.

This file asks one question, again and again. What do the official records show, and what do they leave open?

How this works

Map the years from 2017 to 2025. Compare what the official papers said with what the other records show. Decode the words. Then investigate four questions. In each one, read what is agreed, ask three witnesses the same three questions, and write your finding.

What did they say? Could they really know? Why might they say it? “We can’t know” is always an allowed answer, if you can say why.

    The 2023 referendum

    What did voters officially approve or reject, and what did the Australian Electoral Commission record?

    What is agreed

    • In 2023 voters were asked to approve a proposed law “to alter the Constitution to recognise the First Peoples of Australia by establishing an Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Voice” (AEC, Your official referendum pamphlet, 2023).
    • A change needed a national majority and a majority in at least four of the six States.
    • National enrolment was 17,671,784.
    • The Australian Electoral Commission recorded the national result as Yes 39.94 per cent and No 60.06 per cent, with no State recording a majority of Yes votes.
    • The vote was held on 14 October 2023 (Australian Electoral Commission; this date was confirmed from the AEC for this file).

    Your official referendum pamphlet

    Australian Electoral Commission, 2023

    Source. Australian Electoral Commission, Your official referendum pamphlet (2023), wording of the proposed law.

    See the source (AEC pamphlet, PDF) ↗

    What did they say they saw? “to alter the Constitution to recognise the First Peoples of Australia by establishing an Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Voice.”

    Could they really have seen it? It is the official pamphlet issued for the referendum. It states the wording of the proposed law. This file does not use the Yes and No case arguments from the same pamphlet.

    Why might they say it? It is the official paper that told enrolled voters what they were being asked to approve.

    What a change needed

    The double majority, as recorded by the AEC

    Source. Book Four, citing the Australian Electoral Commission; AEC Tally Room, 2023 Referendum, “Things to know”.

    See the source (AEC Tally Room) ↗

    What did they say they saw? A change needed a national majority and a majority in at least four of the six States. The AEC’s Tally Room states: “For the referendum to pass, the proposed alteration must be approved by: a national majority of voters, and a majority of voters in a majority of the states (at least four out of the six states).”

    Could they really have seen it? This is the official rule for a constitutional referendum to pass, as published by the AEC. Territory results are recorded, but they count in the national total only.

    Why might they say it? It tells voters and later readers what would have been required for the proposed law to pass.

    1967 and 2023

    What do two official referendum figures show, and what can they not show?

    What is agreed

    • In 1967, 90.77 per cent of formal votes had been Yes (Parliamentary Handbook, “Referendums: Aboriginals”).
    • In 2023, the Australian Electoral Commission recorded Yes 39.94 per cent and No 60.06 per cent, with no State recording a majority of Yes votes.
    • The two votes asked different official questions. The 1967 question, as printed in the Parliamentary Handbook, was about altering the Constitution “so as to omit certain words relating to the people of the Aboriginal race in any state so that Aboriginals are to be counted in reckoning the population”. The 2023 proposed law was “to alter the Constitution to recognise the First Peoples of Australia by establishing an Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Voice.”

    The 1967 result

    Parliamentary Handbook

    Source. Parliamentary Handbook, “Referendums: Aboriginals”. Book Four cites this source for the figure 90.77 per cent. The official question printed below is from the same Handbook page.

    See the source (Parliamentary Handbook) ↗

    What did they say they saw? In 1967, 90.77 per cent of formal votes had been Yes. The Handbook records the question as: “Do you approve the proposed law for the alteration of the Constitution entitled ‘An Act to alter the Constitution so as to omit certain words relating to the people of the Aboriginal race in any state so that Aboriginals are to be counted in reckoning the population’?” The Handbook also records that the proposal was carried, with a Yes majority in all six States, on 27 May 1967.

    Could they really have seen it? It is the Parliament’s official handbook of referendum results. The Yes figure is the one already printed in Book Four. The question wording and the date come from the same official page.

    Why might they say it? It is a compiled official record of what was asked, and of the result.

    What did they say they saw? “National · Yes 39.94 per cent · No 60.06 per cent”
    “States with a majority of Yes votes: none of six”

    Could they really have seen it? These are the Commission’s final national figures for 2023. They can be set beside the 1967 figure, but they are results of a different official question.

    Why might they say it? The AEC’s role is to record the result of the vote that was held.

    The Parliamentary Handbook, 1967, and the AEC pamphlet, 2023

    Source. Parliamentary Handbook, “Referendums: Aboriginals”; Australian Electoral Commission, Your official referendum pamphlet (2023).

    Yoorrook, 2021 to 2025

    What do the official Victorian records say the Yoorrook Justice Commission was, and what did it deliver?

    What is agreed

    • In May 2021 the Victorian Government, in partnership with the First Peoples’ Assembly of Victoria, set up the Yoorrook Justice Commission, a Royal Commission (Victorian Government, “Truth and Justice in Victoria”).
    • Yoorrook is the Wemba Wemba and Wamba Wamba word for truth.
    • Four of its five commissioners were First Peoples at all times.
    • Over four years it received more than 1,300 submissions and heard more than 200 witnesses on 65 public hearing days.
    • Its final report, Yoorrook: Truth Be Told, an official public record of First Peoples’ experiences of injustice since the start of colonisation in Victoria, was delivered in June 2025 and released on 1 July 2025. Its reports made 148 recommendations.

    Victorian Government, May 2021

    Source. Victorian Government, First Peoples – State Relations, “Truth and Justice in Victoria”.

    See the source (Victorian Government) ↗

    What did they say they saw? In May 2021 the Victorian Government, in partnership with the First Peoples’ Assembly of Victoria, set up the Yoorrook Justice Commission, a Royal Commission.     Could they really have seen it? This is the Victorian Government’s own public account of the Commission’s establishment. This file uses the published meaning of the name only, and does not retell any further story.
